**Policy client and context:** This brief advises the Australian Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR) on its labour-market response following the 2026 AI and Employment in Australia monitoring report, contributing to the implementation of the National AI Plan’s “Spread the benefits” workforce and Small and Medium Enterprise (SME) adoption agenda (DEWR 2026).

# Managing Australia’s Uneven AI Labour-Market Transition

# Executive Summary

Generative AI presents Australia with a labour-market adjustment challenge rather than evidence of widespread technological unemployment. The DEWR finds no broad AI-driven labour-market upheaval, although employment growth has been slower in occupations with greater automation exposure. At the same time, SME AI adoption remains uneven across firms, while weak productivity growth increases the importance of capturing potential productivity gains. This brief evaluates three responses: targeted worker training, Small-and-Medium Enterprise (SME) AI and workforce co-investment, and early transition assistance. It recommends a two-year, $50 million SME AI and Workforce Co-investment pilot supporting approximately 3,000 firms through 50:50 matched grants. Linking AI adoption to employee training and job redesign would address firm capability constraints while strengthening workers’ complementary skills. Implementation should be evaluated against productivity, skill use, employee retention, and additionality before any national expansion.

# 1. Problem Definition and Policy Context

Generative AI changes labour demand by altering tasks within jobs. It may automate some tasks while augmenting others, and JSA finds that augmentation currently outweighs automation, with automation potential concentrated in routine roles (JSA 2025). DEWR’s July 2026 report finds no broad AI-driven labour-market upheaval, although occupations with greater automation exposure have experienced slower employment growth. DEWR cautions that this evidence is suggestive and does not establish large-scale AI-driven job loss (DEWR 2026).

The net employment effect is therefore uncertain. Automation may reduce labour demand for existing tasks, while productivity gains can expand output and create demand for complementary or new tasks. The policy challenge is whether firms and workers can adjust efficiently. This matters because Australian labour productivity remained barely 1% above its 2015–19 average in the June 2026 quarter (Robson 2026). Policy should therefore support productive AI adoption while improving firm capability and worker reskilling.

# 2. Analysis of Impacts

## 2.1 Tasks, Employment and Productivity

Generative AI changes employment by altering tasks within jobs. Some tasks may be automated, reducing labour demand, while others are augmented as AI raises worker productivity. Exposure therefore does not automatically imply job loss. The overall effect depends on displacement, output expansion and the creation of new tasks (JSA 2025; OECD 2023). Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond found that a generative AI assistant increased productivity by about 15% among 5,172 customer-support agents, with larger gains for less-experienced and lower-skilled workers. Because the study examined one firm, it does not establish economy-wide employment or wage effects (Brynjolfsson et al. 2025). Australian evidence currently suggests adjustment rather than widespread displacement. JSA reports changing roles, upskilling and redeployment, while around 15% of workers change occupations each year. This shows that AI is entering an already dynamic labour market, not that AI causes this mobility (JSA 2025).
## 2.2 Wages and Distribution
Higher productivity does not automatically produce higher wages. Gains may flow to workers, firms or consumers. AI substitution may weaken demand for some tasks, while augmentation may increase the value of complementary skills (Productivity Commission 2024; OECD 2023). Distributional effects are therefore uneven. Less-experienced workers may gain from augmentation, while workers performing more automatable tasks may face greater transition risks. Access to retraining and AI-complementary skills will influence who captures productivity gains and who bears adjustment costs (Brynjolfsson et al. 2025; JSA 2025; OECD 2024).
## 2.3 Industry Structure and Uneven Firm Adjustment
ABS reports that 12% of Australian businesses used AI in 2024–25. Adoption ranged from 38% in Information, Media and Telecommunications to 1% in Transport, Postal and Warehousing. Among innovation-active firms, use ranged from 18% for micro firms to 37% for large firms (ABS 2026). These differences may contribute to productivity and capability gaps. OECD evidence suggests that AI benefits depend on complementary investment in infrastructure, management, workforce skills and organisational change (OECD 2024). Smaller firms may therefore face greater constraints, making the adjustment challenge not only worker reskilling but also whether SMEs can adopt AI in ways that build workforce capability.

# 3. Policy Options and Evaluation
The three options address adjustment frictions, uneven AI adoption and underinvestment in workforce skills. 

**Option 1:** Targeted AI Transition Training. This option would subsidise approved AI and digital training for workers in occupations facing high AI exposure or changing task demand. It targets workers with limited access to employer-funded learning or greater transition risk (JSA 2026). A two-year pilot for 20,000 workers, with subsidies of up to $3,000 each, would cost about $66 million, including administration and evaluation.

**Option 2:** SME AI and Workforce Co-investment. This option would provide matched grants to SMEs that adopt AI while also investing in employee training and job redesign. It targets smaller firms facing financial, skills and organisational constraints (DISR 2025). A two-year pilot supporting around 3,000 firms with an average Commonwealth contribution of $15,000 would cost about $50 million, including administration and evaluation.

**Option 3:** AI Early Transition Service. This option would use JSA and DEWR indicators to identify occupations where high AI exposure coincides with weakening employment or vacancy trends. Affected workers would receive skills assessment, career guidance and job matching (DEWR n.d). A two-year pilot supporting around 20,000 workers would cost approximately $20–25 million, including data and evaluation costs.

**Table 1:** _Comparison of 3 Policy Options_

| Criterion | Option 1: Targeted AI Transition Training | Option 2: SME AI and Workforce Co-investment | Option 3: AI Early Transition Service |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Efficiency** | Directly addresses skill gaps, but outcomes depend on training relevance and may involve deadweight spending. | Addresses AI adoption and workforce capability together, but some firms may have invested anyway. | Reduces search and information frictions, but does not address firm capability or major retraining needs. |
| **Equity** | Targets workers facing greater transition risks. | Broadens AI access for SMEs and their employees. | Targets workers experiencing observable labour-market deterioration. |
| **Feasibility** | Can use existing training providers. | Can build on existing SME AI support infrastructure. | Can use existing employment-service infrastructure. |
| **Fiscal cost** | Highest, about **$66 million**. | Moderate, about **$50 million** with private co-funding. | Lowest, about **$20–25 million**. |

The main trade-off is between timing, targeting and cost. Option 1 supports workers directly but is the most expensive and depends on training quality. Option 3 is cheapest and more targeted, but reacts only after deterioration becomes visible. Option 2 intervenes earlier and links AI adoption with workforce development, but carries additionality risk. Compared with Option 1, it targets workers less directly but better addresses the firm-level adoption gap identified in Section 2. 
# 4. Recommended Package and Implementation Plan
The recommended package is Option 2: SME AI and Workforce Co-investment because it directly addresses the uneven firm adoption and workforce capability gaps. The program would provide 50:50 matched grants to SMEs that adopt AI while also investing in employee training and job redesign (Australian Government n.d). A two-year pilot supporting around 3,000 firms, with an average Commonwealth contribution of $15,000, would cost approximately $50 million, including administration and evaluation.

**Phase 1 - Design, months 0–6:** DEWR, supported by JSA, would establish eligibility rules, additionality tests, approved training requirements and baseline productivity measures. Around $3 million would be allocated to program design, data systems and application setup. Key indicators would include completion of the eligibility framework, baseline data coverage and readiness of approved training standards.

**Phase 2 - Pilot delivery, months 6–18:** DEWR would award competitive grants, while participating SMEs would provide matching private funds and implement AI adoption, training and job redesign plans. Around $44 million would fund grants and delivery support. KPIs would include the number of firms implementing AI, worker training completion, use of new skills at work, and employee retention.

**Phase 3 - Evaluation and decision, months 18–24:** An independent evaluator would compare participating firms with matched non-participants using baseline and follow-up measures. Around $3 million would support evaluation and final reporting. KPIs would include productivity change, the proportion of investment assessed as additional, and differences in outcomes between participating and comparison firms. DEWR would then decide whether to scale, redesign, or discontinue the program.

# 5. Risks and Mitigations 

The proposed program faces three main implementation risks that should be addressed through its design and evaluation. The first risk is deadweight spending. Some SMEs may receive grants for AI investments they would have undertaken without government support, reducing value for money. This risk should be limited through 50:50 co-funding, additionality tests, and evidence that the grant changes the timing, scale, or workforce component of the investment. Firms unable to demonstrate additional activity should not receive funding. The second risk is poor training quality or weak relevance to workplace needs. Training that is too generic may increase completion rates without improving worker capability or productivity. Eligible training should therefore be linked directly to the firm’s AI adoption and job-redesign plan. Providers should also demonstrate practical workplace application, with evaluation focusing on skill use and productivity outcomes rather than training completion alone.
